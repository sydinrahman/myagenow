The Math Behind Leap Years: Why Our Calendar Needs Adjusting

Published on October 27, 2026 • 7 min read • By My Age Now team

Every four years, February gets an extra day. Most of us accept this without a second thought, but the leap day isn't a quirk or a tradition. It's a correction, and the math behind it explains why our calendar would slowly drift away from the seasons without it. In this guide you'll see exactly how big the problem is, how the Julian and Gregorian calendars tried to fix it, and why it matters when you calculate your age.

The problem: a year isn't a whole number of days

A calendar year has a whole number of days, but the natural cycle it tries to follow does not. The tropical year is the time between one spring equinox and the next, and it lasts about 365.2422 days, or roughly 365 days, 5 hours, 48 minutes and 45 seconds.

A 365-day calendar therefore falls short by about 0.2422 days each year, close to six hours. That sounds tiny, but it adds up, and the seasons stay tied to the tropical year, not to our calendar.

How fast the calendar would drift

If we never added leap days, every year would end about 0.2422 days earlier than the sun says it should. Here is how that accumulates:

Years elapsed Calculation Approximate drift
4 years 4 × 0.2422 about 1 day
100 years 100 × 0.2422 about 24 days
400 years 400 × 0.2422 about 97 days
700 years 700 × 0.2422 about 170 days (more than five months)

After roughly seven centuries, July in the northern hemisphere would feel like winter. That is the entire reason leap years exist: to pull the calendar back in step with the seasons.

The Julian fix and its flaw

A simple rule from 45 BCE

In 45 BCE, Julius Caesar introduced the Julian calendar. Its rule was easy: add a leap day every fourth year. Over four years that gives 365 × 3 + 366 = 1,461 days, so the average Julian year is 1,461 ÷ 4 = 365.25 days.

Why it still drifted

365.25 is close to 365.2422, but not close enough. The difference is 365.25 − 365.2422 = 0.0078 days per year, about 11 minutes. That error works out to roughly one extra day every 128 years. By the 1500s the calendar had slipped around ten days behind the equinox.

The Gregorian rules, step by step

In 1582, Pope Gregory XIII introduced a reformed calendar. It did two things: it skipped ten days to realign the date (in countries that adopted it first, October 4 was followed directly by October 15), and it changed the leap year rule so the drift would stop building up.

The modern rule has three parts. A year is a leap year if:

  1. It is divisible by 4, and
  2. It is not divisible by 100, unless
  3. It is also divisible by 400.

Over a 400-year cycle this gives 100 leap years minus 3 skipped century years = 97 leap years. The total is 400 × 365 + 97 = 146,097 days, and 146,097 ÷ 400 = 365.2425 days per year on average.

Compare that with the real value of about 365.2422. The remaining gap is around 0.0003 days a year, so the Gregorian calendar slips by only about one day every few thousand years.

Why 1900 wasn't a leap year but 2000 was

The century exception is what trips most people up. Use this table to test any year:

Year ÷ 4? ÷ 100? ÷ 400? Leap year?
1900 Yes Yes No No
2000 Yes Yes Yes Yes
2024 Yes No n/a Yes
2026 No n/a n/a No
2028 Yes No n/a Yes
2100 Yes Yes No No

Skipping three leap days every 400 years is exactly what trims the average from 365.25 down to 365.2425.

Leap days and age calculation

Leap years also create an interesting question for anyone born on February 29: when is your birthday in a year without one? Since 4 × 365 + 1 = 1,461 days make up a normal four-year cycle, only about 1 in 1,461 people is born on a leap day.

There is no single universal answer. Many calculators and some jurisdictions treat the birthday as February 28 in non-leap years, while others use March 1. Here is how the two conventions differ for someone born on February 29, 2000:

Date in 2026 Age if birthday = Feb 28 Age if birthday = Mar 1
February 27 25 25
February 28 26 25
March 1 26 26

The two rules only disagree for a single day, February 28. For everything else, the age in completed years comes out the same. Leap days also change totals: a person who has lived through several leap years has lived more days than "age × 365", which is why exact day counts should always use real calendar dates.

Frequently asked questions

Why do we need leap years?
Earth needs about 365.2422 days to complete one cycle of the seasons, while a calendar year has a whole number of days. Adding a leap day roughly every four years keeps the calendar aligned with the seasons.

How do you know if a year is a leap year?
A year is a leap year if it is divisible by 4, except for century years, which must also be divisible by 400. So 2024 and 2000 are leap years, while 1900 and 2100 are not.

Was 1900 a leap year?
No. 1900 is divisible by 4 and 100 but not by 400, so it was a common 365-day year under the Gregorian calendar.

How often does a leap day happen?
Usually every four years. The Gregorian calendar has 97 leap years in each 400-year cycle, giving an average year of 365.2425 days.

When is the next leap year?
After 2024, the next leap years are 2028 and 2032. The year 2026 is not a leap year.

How is age calculated for someone born on February 29?
Conventions differ: many calculators and some jurisdictions use February 28 in non-leap years, while others use March 1. Either way, age in completed years goes up by one each year.

Key takeaways

  • The tropical year is about 365.2422 days, so a 365-day calendar drifts about six hours per year.
  • The Julian calendar (365.25 days) overcorrected slightly and drifted about one day every 128 years.
  • The Gregorian rule (÷4, except centuries unless ÷400) averages 365.2425 days and stays accurate for thousands of years.
  • Leap days affect exact day counts and the Feb 29 birthday question, so use a calendar-aware tool for precise ages.
