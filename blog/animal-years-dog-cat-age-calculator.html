Animal Years: How Do We Actually Calculate Dog and Cat Ages?

Published by myagenow Team • 7 min read • Updated October 2026

“One dog year equals seven human years.” Almost everyone has heard it, and almost everyone has used it. It’s also wrong. Here’s how pet ages are really worked out, which formulas vets and scientists use, and how to get a number you can trust for your own dog or cat.

The Short Answer

Pets don’t age at a steady rate. They shoot through childhood and adolescence in the first two years, then slow down. So the math has to be lopsided too:

  • Dogs (medium size): 15 human years for year one, 9 more for year two, then about 5 for every year after that.
  • Cats: 15 human years for year one, 9 more for year two, then about 4 for every year after that.

That’s a rule of thumb, not a lab result. Below we’ll look at where it comes from, how a newer scientific formula compares, and why your pet’s size and breed shift the answer.

Why “Times Seven” Doesn’t Work

No one knows for sure where the rule started. A popular explanation is that it came from comparing lifespans: humans lived about 70 years and dogs about 10, so you multiply by 7. Neat, but it treats a lifetime as a straight line.

Real bodies don’t work that way. Think about a one-year-old dog. The “times seven” rule says it’s the equivalent of a 7-year-old child. But that dog is fully grown, can reproduce, and has the energy of a teenager. The study that tested this put it simply: a 9-month-old dog can already have puppies, so the multiplication can’t be right.

Dogs: The Vet-Style Formula

The most widely repeated guideline, the one the American Veterinary Medical Association gives pet owners, is built around the idea that early life moves fast. It’s written for a medium-sized dog.

  • Year 1 = 15 human years
  • Year 2 = +9 (total 24)
  • Each year after = +5

How to calculate it, step by step

  1. Take your dog’s age in years. Say it’s 7.
  2. Give the first two years a flat 24 human years (15 + 9).
  3. Count the remaining years: 7 − 2 = 5.
  4. Multiply those by 5: 5 × 5 = 25.
  5. Add them together: 24 + 25 = 49 human years.

As one line of math: human age = 24 + 5 × (dog age − 2), for dogs older than 2.

Dogs: The Epigenetic Clock Formula

In 2020, researchers at the University of California San Diego tried something different. Instead of guessing from life stages, they looked at DNA methylation, chemical changes that build up on DNA as an animal ages. They compared the pattern in 104 Labrador Retrievers with the pattern in humans and came up with this:

human age = 16 × ln(dog age) + 31

The “ln” is the natural logarithm, a button you’ll find on any scientific calculator (your phone has one if you turn it sideways). For a 7-year-old dog: ln(7) is about 1.95, times 16 is about 31.1, plus 31 gives roughly 62 human years.

The curve climbs fast at first and flattens later, which matches what we see in real dogs. It also says a 1-year-old dog is about 31 in human terms, far older than the 15 you’ll see in most charts.

Worth knowing: the study used Labradors only, so the authors themselves noted that other breeds may need their own clocks. Treat the result as a smart estimate, not a verdict.

Notice how much the methods disagree about young dogs and how closely they agree in the middle. The ×7 rule only looks right around age 7, and that’s a coincidence, not a rule. All values are rounded to whole years.

Dogs: Why Size Matters

Here’s the part the simple formula misses. Small dogs often live to 14 or 15 and beyond, while giant breeds like Great Danes may be seniors by age 5 or 6. A 10-year-old Chihuahua and a 10-year-old Great Dane are in very different places.

Many charts handle this by splitting dogs into weight groups. These are approximate values, and different charts vary by a couple of years, so use them as a guide:

Dog’s age Small (under 20 lb) Medium (21–50 lb) Large (51–90 lb) Giant (90+ lb)
1 year 15 15 15 12
2 years 24 24 24 22
10 years 56 60 66 79
15 years 76 83 93 115

The takeaway: the early years look similar for every size, but the gap widens fast after that. If you have a mixed breed, use the size group closest to your dog’s adult weight.

Cats: How the Math Works

Cats are simpler. They follow the same fast start as dogs, then settle into a steady pace of about 4 human years per cat year. This is the guideline used in feline life-stage recommendations from veterinary groups.

  • Year 1 = 15 human years
  • Year 2 = +9 (total 24)
  • Each year after = +4

human age = 24 + 4 × (cat age − 2)

A 5-year-old cat: 24 + 4 × 3 = 36. A 10-year-old cat: 24 + 4 × 8 = 56, which is solidly middle-aged, not a retiree.

Cats don’t come in size classes the way dogs do, which is a big reason one formula works reasonably well for all of them. Lifestyle matters though: indoor cats commonly live well into their teens, while cats that roam outdoors face more risks.

Why No Formula Is Perfect

  • Breed and genetics: Some breeds are simply built to last longer than others.
  • Health and lifestyle: Diet, weight, exercise, and vet care all change how an animal ages.
  • Different goals: A formula based on life stages and one based on DNA are answering slightly different questions.

Use these numbers to understand roughly where your pet is in life. For anything health-related, such as when to start senior check-ups, ask your vet.

Getting Your Pet’s Exact Age First

Every formula above starts with one number: how old your pet is. If you know the birth date, count the full years and months. Four years and six months is 4.5. If you adopted a rescue, ask the shelter for their best estimate. Vets can often judge a dog or cat’s age from teeth and general condition, though only within a rough range.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is one dog year really equal to 7 human years?
No. The 'times seven' rule is a simple myth based on average lifespans. Dogs age much faster in their first two years than they do later in life.

How old is a 1-year-old dog in human years?
According to veterinary guidelines, a 1-year-old dog is roughly equivalent to a 15-year-old human teenager. The epigenetic DNA clock formula estimates a 1-year-old dog at about 31 human years.

How do you calculate a cat’s age in human years?
Cats equal 15 human years for year 1, 9 additional years for year 2 (total 24), and approximately 4 human years for each year after that (Formula: 24 + 4 × (cat age − 2)).

Do small dogs age slower than large dogs?
Yes. While small and large dogs mature at similar rates in their first two years, large and giant dog breeds age significantly faster in their senior years compared to small breeds.

At what age is a dog or cat considered senior?
Cats and small dogs are usually considered senior around 7 to 10 years old, whereas large and giant dog breeds reach senior status by age 5 or 6.

Final Thoughts

The next time someone says “that’s 49 in dog years,” you’ll know the real answer is more interesting. Pets grow up quickly, then age at a slower and steadier pace, and size and breed change the picture. Use the formulas here for a good estimate, and use your vet for the final word.
