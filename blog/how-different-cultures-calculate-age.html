How Different Cultures Around the World Calculate Age Traditionally

Imagine a baby born on December 31. In much of the world, she's zero years old and will stay that way for the next twelve months. In traditional Korea, she's already one on the day she's born. By the next morning, January 1, she turns two. She's one day old and two years old, and nobody in the room thinks that's strange.

Age feels like the most objective number about a person. It isn't. It's a convention, and different cultures settled on different ones. Some count the time in the womb, some add a year at New Year instead of the birthday, and some use calendars that measure the year differently altogether.

Let's walk through the major systems, how they work, and how to convert between them.

The Western method: counting completed years

Most of the world, and almost every legal system, uses what's often called "full age" or "international age." You start at zero, and you add one on each birthday. It counts how many full years you've lived.

It's the easiest to calculate and the easiest to prove, which is why governments, hospitals and schools use it. If you want to check yours, our age calculator uses this method.

East Asian age reckoning

The best-known alternative is the system traditionally used in Korea, China, Japan and Vietnam. The details vary by country, but two ideas run through all of them:

  1. You're born at age one. The time spent in the womb counts as a year of life.
  2. Everyone gets a year older together at New Year, not on their own birthday.

The result is that your traditional age is usually one or two years higher than your international age.

Korea

Korea has three systems, which is where the confusion comes from:

  • Seoul-age (세는 나이): The traditional count. You're 1 at birth and add a year every January 1. The formula is simple: current year − birth year + 1.
  • Year-age (연 나이): You're 0 at birth and add a year every January 1. The formula is current year − birth year.
  • International age (만 나이): The standard birthday-based count.

In June 2023, South Korea changed its civil and administrative law so that international age became the official standard for most legal purposes. The goal was to cut down on confusion and legal disputes. People still use the traditional count in casual conversation, though, and a few areas, like some age limits for alcohol, tobacco and military service, continue to use year-age.

China

In China, the traditional age is called xusui (虚岁), or "nominal age." You're one at birth, and you add a year at the Lunar New Year, not on January 1. Because the Lunar New Year moves between late January and mid-February, the date changes every year.

For example, a baby born in December 2025 would be one at birth and two on Lunar New Year, which fell on February 17 in 2026. Modern China uses the full-age count (周岁) for official purposes, while the traditional count survives in family conversation, folk customs and astrology.

Japan

Japan's version is called kazoedoshi (数え年), or "counted age." It follows the same logic: one at birth, plus a year every New Year. Japan moved away from it in the early 20th century and officially encouraged the Western method in 1950.

It hasn't vanished. Kazoedoshi still shows up in traditional events, and especially in yakudoshi, the "unlucky years" thought to bring bad fortune. Many people go to a shrine for a purification ritual when they reach one of these ages, which are counted traditionally.

Vietnam

In Vietnam, the traditional age is called tuổi mụ. Again, you're one at birth, and you add a year at Tết, the Lunar New Year. Like elsewhere, official documents use the standard count, and tuổi mụ lives on in family customs.

Not every culture counts differently, but some use different calendars

Another reason people's ages differ across cultures is that the year itself isn't always the same length, or doesn't start at the same point.

Islamic (Hijri) calendar. It's a purely lunar calendar with about 354 days a year, roughly 11 days shorter than a Gregorian year. As a result, a person's age in Hijri years grows a little faster. Someone who is 30 in Gregorian years is about 31 in Hijri years, and the gap widens as you get older.

Thai solar calendar. Thailand uses the Buddhist Era, which is the Gregorian year plus 543. The year 2026 is 2569 in the Buddhist Era. The years are the same length, so age doesn't change, but you'll see birth years on Thai documents that look unfamiliar.

Nepal's Bikram Sambat. It runs about 56 to 57 years ahead of the Gregorian calendar, so birth years on Nepali documents look very different from their Gregorian equivalents. Age is still calculated from the birth date, but you need to convert the year first.

Ethiopian calendar. It runs about seven to eight years behind the Gregorian one, and has thirteen months. Birth dates on local documents may need converting before you can calculate age in the international system.

Iran's Solar Hijri calendar. It's a solar calendar numbered from 622 CE, so the year number is about 621 or 622 less than the Gregorian one.

In all of these cases, the person is the same age in real elapsed time. The numbers on the paper just don't match.

How to convert traditional age to international age

Here are some rules of thumb. They're approximations, because the exact answer depends on the birth date:

  • Korean Seoul-age to international age: Subtract 1 or 2. Subtract 1 if the birthday has already passed this year, and 2 if it hasn't.
  • Chinese xusui to international age: Subtract 1 or 2, depending on whether the Lunar New Year and the birthday have passed.
  • Japanese kazoedoshi to international age: Same logic. Subtract 1 or 2.

The reliable method is to avoid shortcuts. Start from the actual birth date and count completed years from there. If the birth date is only known in a lunar or other calendar, convert it to the Gregorian date first, then calculate the age.

Why this matters in real life

If you work with international forms, immigration paperwork, medical records, insurance or school enrollment, age mismatches create real problems. A document from a Korean family in 2022 might list a child's Seoul-age, while a form here asks for international age. Mixing them up can mean the wrong age bracket on a form.

It also matters for something simpler, like understanding a friend. If someone tells you their grandmother is "ninety," ask whether she counts the traditional way. She might be eighty-eight.

Quick takeaways

  • The Western method counts completed years, so you start at 0 and add one on each birthday.
  • East Asian reckoning starts at 1 and adds a year at New Year, either January 1 (Korea's Seoul-age) or the Lunar New Year (China, Vietnam).
  • Korea made international age the legal standard for most purposes in June 2023, but the old count still appears in daily life.
  • Different calendars (Hijri, Buddhist Era, Bikram Sambat, Ethiopian) change the year numbers, so convert birth dates before calculating age.
  • When accuracy matters, always calculate from the exact birth date.

FAQ

What is East Asian age reckoning?
A traditional system where you're considered one year old at birth and gain a year at New Year instead of on your birthday.

Is Korean age still used?
Informally, yes. In 2023, South Korea made international age the standard for most legal and administrative purposes, but the traditional count remains common in conversation.

Why are you one year old at birth in some cultures?
The traditional count includes the time spent in the womb as the first year of life.

How do I convert Korean age to international age?
Subtract 1 or 2 from the Seoul-age. Subtract 1 if you've already had your birthday this year, or 2 if you haven't.

Do all cultures add a year on the birthday?
No. Some add it at New Year, and some use calendars with different year lengths.
